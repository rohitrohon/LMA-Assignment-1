================================================================================
1. Architecture Comparison
================================================================================
A Bi-Encoder (here, sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2) maps the query and each chunk into independent 384-dimensional vectors. The similarity score is simply the dot product or cosine angle between the two vectors. Because chunk vectors are completely independent of the query, the entire corpus can be embedded offline and stored in a vector index (e.g. FAISS). At runtime, search is nearly instantaneous (0.03 ms for 73 chunks, 6.2 ms for 100,000 vectors). The architectural cost is an information bottleneck: condensing an entire 180-word passage into a single dense vector compresses away subtle nuances such as negative constraints ("may NOT trade"), conditional requirements ("only on your turn"), and specific exception clauses.

A Cross-Encoder (cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) receives the concatenated pair [CLS] query [SEP] chunk [SEP] as a single input and runs full all-to-all cross-attention across all transformer layers. Every query token directly attends to every document token, enabling the model to distinguish true semantic relevance from superficial keyword overlap. This delivers substantially higher ranking accuracy, but nothing can be precomputed offline: evaluating N chunks requires N separate forward passes through the transformer for every single query.

Detailed Query Walkthroughs:

1. "Does my longest road still count if an opponent builds a settlement in the middle of it?" (road_broken)
- Chunk 33 (p. 9, Longest Road break & tie rule) jumped from rank #5 to #2 (+3).
- Baseline top 3: Chunks 32, 12, 48. Re-ranked top 3: Chunks 32, 33, 12.
- Baseline Answer 1: Misstatement. Claimed the player "must give the special card to another player", confusing the general takeover rule with an automatic transfer, and missed that continuous segments must be recounted.
- Re-ranked Answer 2: Accurate. Stated that the road splits, continuous lengths must be recounted, and correctly retained the tie rule from Chunk 33 (incumbent keeps the card if still tied).

2. "How do I make my settlement bigger and what extra do I get from it?" (bigger_settlement)
- Chunk 13 (p. 5, City upgrade, cost, 2-card production) surged from rank #7 to #2 (+5).
- Baseline top 3: Chunks 53, 71, 4. The word "settlement" acted as a keyword trap, pulling in setup and distance rules.
- Baseline Answer 1: Severe omission. Stated only that a settlement upgrades to a city for 2 victory points, omitting the resource cost (3 ore, 2 grain) and the double production benefit entirely.
- Re-ranked Answer 2: Complete. Explicitly provided the upgrade cost (3 Ore, 2 Grain), noted piece replacement, and explained that cities produce 2 resource cards per adjacent producing hex.
(Note: All cross-encoder scores for this query were negative, peak -1.09, showing low raw confidence due to the informal phrasing "make bigger", but the relative ordering correctly promoted the target chunk).

3. "Can I swap my resource cards for something I need without involving other players?" (bank_trade_synonym)
- Chunk 34 (p. 9, Maritime trade & harbor ratios) moved from rank #4 to #1 (+3).
- Baseline top 3: Chunks 9, 40, 38. Pulled heavily toward domestic trade with other players.
- Baseline Answer 1: Incomplete. Confirmed maritime trade is possible ("trade without the other players"), but gave zero exchange rates.
- Re-ranked Answer 2: Precise. Provided the full schedule: 4:1 bank rate, 3:1 generic harbor, and 2:1 specialized harbor.

4. "Can two players who are not taking their turn trade resources with each other?" (trade_negation)
- Chunk 10 (p. 4, restriction that only active player may trade) moved from rank #8 to #1 (+7).
- Both baseline and re-ranked answers were correct.
- Why baseline succeeded: Due to the 40-word sliding window chunk overlap, the last 40 words of Chunk 9 (baseline rank #1) are identical to the first 40 words of Chunk 10. The restriction sentence was present in Chunk 9 by sheer overlap, demonstrating how sliding windows can mask retrieval failures.

5. Controls:
- "I hit 10 victory points on someone else's turn, do I win?": Chunk 26 stayed rank #1, both answers correct (only on your own turn).
- "How many knights for Largest Army?": Chunk 16 moved from #2 to #1, both answers correct (3 knights).
- Custom query (15 cards: 13 resource, 2 dev cards on a 7-roll): Chunk 44 moved from #2 to #1, both answers correctly calculated discarding 6 resource cards (13 / 2 rounded down, dev cards excluded).

================================================================================
4. Scaling Question
================================================================================
Why can we not run the Cross-Encoder over the entire document collection?

In a Cross-Encoder, the query and passage are concatenated before inference. Because every input depends on the query, inference cost is strictly O(N) transformer forward passes per query, with zero precomputation possible.

Empirical measurement: Running the cross-encoder over all 73 chunks in the rulebook took 1.24 s (~17.0 ms per pair).
Projected throughput at larger corpus sizes:
- 1,000 chunks:     17.0 s (~0.3 min)
- 10,000 chunks:    170 s (~2.8 min)
- 100,000 chunks:   1,700 s (~28.3 min)
- 1,000,000 chunks: 16,998 s (~4.7 hours per single query)

By contrast, a FAISS flat search over 100,000 synthetic 384-dimensional vectors took just 6.2 ms (~274,000x faster).

This necessitates the standard two-stage retrieval cascade:
1. Stage 1: A fast Bi-Encoder filters millions of chunks down to Top K (e.g. 10 to 50) in milliseconds.
2. Stage 2: A Cross-Encoder performs deep cross-attention on only those K candidates in constant time O(K).
Limitation: If the Bi-Encoder fails to retrieve the correct chunk into the top K candidates, the Cross-Encoder can never recover it.
